Mechanical design and simulation

University projects where I designed something, simulated it, and in most cases built it.

My part: the actuator

Our group of six designed a small robot that sits on a billiard table, aims and takes the shot by itself. Each of us had a named role. Mine was Magnetics Designer: I was responsible for the actuator that hits the ball.

The design

We picked a voice coil over pneumatic and spring actuators, mostly for its size, mass and simplicity. I designed it with a 1018 steel yoke, a bonded NdFeB magnet (much cheaper than sintered and strong enough) and a 100-turn coil running at 1 A.

What the simulation gave

I simulated it in FEMM over several versions and used MATLAB to pull the flux along the coil. From that I worked out the force, inductance, resistance and current density, and checked them against similar actuators from Thorlabs and Moticont. Closing the air gap took the force from 0.8 to 1.05 N per amp.

Keeping a print head steady

The carriage on Canon's Colorado 1650 holds the print heads, and it has to drop ink to within 20 µm while the printer around it vibrates at about 50 Hz. Our group of six was asked to stiffen it so its natural frequencies sat well clear of that, while adding no more than 1.5 kg.

My contribution

  • Worked out, with a teammate, how to get the natural frequencies out of NX: a modal analysis followed by a frequency response run.
  • Designed three of the stiffening parts: a front part running along the carriage, a mid-side reinforcement and a new backplate. All are 1.5 mm steel sheet bolted to the side plates; I meshed each one and connected it into the model.
  • Put the final assembly together with a teammate, combining everyone's parts into one model and getting the connections to actually hold. A lot of my time went into solver and meshing errors, and fixing them taught me more about how NX builds a model than any tutorial did.
  • Wrote the FE validation part of the report: why we used 2D shell meshes over 3D for sheet metal, and how we modelled the bolted joints.

As far as possible, on a falling weight

The brief: launch a 50 g foam plane as far as possible, powered only by a falling counterweight and a drum. Every printed part had to come out of 10% of a 300 × 100 × 50 mm box. The group went with a slider on a double drum, which multiplies the pull of the counterweight.

My contribution

  • Designed my own version of the mechanism in NX: a single support on a hollow base connection, built to use as little print volume as possible. The group went with a two-support design instead, but I still like mine.
  • Ran a Simcenter motion simulation of the final mechanism to estimate the launch speed across launch angles.
  • Wrote the trajectory model in MATLAB, adding lift and drag to plain projectile motion using coefficients we measured. Getting it to behave took several rebuilds.
  • For the CAD part of the course, got our pendulum clock simulation working. The escape wheel turned out to be spinning the wrong way.

How it turned out

Indicators for bikes

Cars have indicators and brake lights. Bikes, mostly, don't. Our group of six designed a kit that clips onto any bike: LED strips front and back for indicators and daytime lights, buttons on the handlebar, an accelerometer that lights up when you brake, and a buzzer that goes off if you crash.

My contribution

The electronics and code were mine.

  • Chose the components and kept them within budget.
  • Wrote the Arduino logic for the indicators, the brake light and crash detection.
  • Drew up the circuit schematics so everything would fit in the enclosures.
  • Did the soldering and wiring.
  • Debugged what worked in simulation but not on the bike. The LED strips took one data pin instead of three, and the latching buttons were a puzzle to wire.
  • Early on, modelled the first electronics enclosures in CAD.

How it turned out

Every feature worked on the finished bike, and the indicators were bright and obvious. It wasn't waterproof, the wiring was rough, and in hindsight the brake light should have been red, not green.
