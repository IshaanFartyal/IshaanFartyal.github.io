Bachelor's Thesis · CFD · Thermo-Fluids

Finding the Transition Plane in Gas Atomization

Simulating how molten metal breaks into powder takes two numerical models, one after the other. I analyzed droplet data from two simulations to find the earliest point where the first model can safely hand over to the second.

Read the thesis ↗

Bachelor Final Project, Mechanical Engineering (Power & Flow), Eindhoven University of Technology, August 2026.

The recommendation
20 mm downstream of the nozzle:
the recommended transition plane
01 Situation

Metal powder starts as a spray

Metal 3D printing needs fine, uniform powder. That powder is made by gas atomization: a high-speed gas jet tears a stream of molten metal into droplets, which cool into particles.

Simulating this takes two models. Close to the nozzle, a detailed but expensive model (Volume-of-Fluid) resolves the metal stream as it breaks apart. Further down, a cheaper model treats each droplet as a point. A transition plane is where one hands over to the other.

02 Question

Too early is unreliable. Too late is expensive.

Hand over too close to the nozzle and the droplets are not yet small and stable enough to be treated as points. Hand over too far away and the expensive model runs longer than it needs to. The question was where the earliest reliable location is.

03 Approach

Follow the droplets downstream

I analyzed the output of two OpenFOAM simulations that differ in metal, gas and nozzle geometry: aluminium in nitrogen, and copper-tin in argon. Every detected droplet was sorted into 1 mm slices by its distance from the nozzle.

The raw data is noisy, so I used medians and interquartile ranges instead of averages, then tracked how droplet size, speed, temperature and shape change with distance.

04 Result

A plane at 20 mm, and a rule for finding it

12 mm

The lower limit. The unbroken metal stream reaches this far, and the plane cannot cut through it.

15–20 mm

Where both cases settle into the same trends: speed and temperature from 15 mm, size from 20 mm.

That gives 20 mm for the cases studied. The more useful result is the general rule: place the plane at the earliest point where droplet size and temperature are falling and speed is rising, and never closer than the length of the unbroken stream.

What stayed inconclusive

Four measures of droplet roundness and the Weber number showed no consistent trend across the two cases, so I did not build the recommendation on them. The 20 mm figure applies to these two cases, not to gas atomization in general.
