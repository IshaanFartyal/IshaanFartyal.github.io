ASML Technology Strategy

ASML leads the market for the machines that print the world's most advanced chips. I analysed its business, finances and risks to answer one question: where should it focus next to stay ahead?

01 Situation

A company with very little competition

ASML builds lithography systems, the machines chipmakers such as TSMC, Intel and Samsung use to pattern chips. In EUV, the technology behind the most advanced chips, it faces limited direct competition, and the numbers show it.

€32.7bn

Revenue in 2025, up from €18.6bn in 2021. That is growth of about 15% a year.

50–53%

Gross margin over the five years, against roughly 47–48% for its main equipment peers.

25%

Share of 2025 sales that came from servicing and upgrading machines already installed.

14–15%

Share of revenue reinvested in R&D each year, reaching €4.7bn in 2025.

02 Question

A strong position is not a safe one

Staying ahead depends on three things ASML does not fully control.

Technology High-NA EUV

Its next-generation machine must pay off for customers, not only work.

Geopolitics Export controls

China, Taiwan and South Korea made up about 80% of 2025 sales.

Supply chain Specialised suppliers

Critical parts come from a few suppliers that must scale with it.

So the question was: how should ASML rank its strategic priorities to keep its lead through the next generation of lithography?

03 Approach

From the business model to three options

I worked through ASML's business model, its competitive position and five years of annual-report financials. That led to three courses of action, which I compared on value, cost, risk and timing.

Option Benefit Value Investment Risk
Accelerate High-NA adoption Extends the technology lead Very high High Medium
Earn more from the installed base More recurring revenue Medium Medium Low
Selective vertical integration A more resilient supply chain High Very high High
04 Recommendation

High-NA first, then the installed base, then suppliers

  1. Make High-NA pay for customers. ASML's advantage rests on technology leadership. The priority is throughput, availability and total cost of ownership, because customers adopt a new machine when the economics work.
  2. Use the installed base as the steady earner. Service, upgrades and field options bring lower-risk recurring revenue. They should support investment in new technology, not replace it.
  3. Integrate only where supply could hold High-NA back. Broad vertical integration would be costly and inefficient. Target the few components where supplier concentration is a real risk.
The main risk

High-NA adoption could be slower than expected, leaving a large upfront investment waiting for returns. Export controls are treated as a constraint across all three options, since ASML has little control over them.
